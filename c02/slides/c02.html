Today: the loss

Why cosine cannot be the loss

Suppose we train the average model to make the cosine larger.

1. A cosine of 1 is not the goal

A cosine of 1 means the article has the same mix of words as its topic average. We never wanted that.

2. Cosine does not move away from the rivals

A learning model should move toward the true topic and away from the false topics. Cosine scores each topic on its own. A larger cosine for the true topic leaves the other cosines as they are.

We need probabilities

Each news item belongs to one topic. Our goal is to be fully confident of that topic: p(\text{true topic} \mid \text{article}) = 1.

The six probabilities add up to 1. When the true topic's probability rises, the probabilities of the other topics fall.

Each word multiplies the product by about 0.002.

Problem: by word 120 the product is below the smallest positive 64-bit number, so it becomes 0.

The log turns the product into a sum, and the sum stays in range.

The log is increasing, so the topic with the largest product has the largest score.

To get probabilities back, subtract the largest score, take the exponential and divide by the sum. This is softmax.

Solved: compute with sums of logs, and turn the scores into probabilities with softmax.

Naive Bayes with logs

Word probabilities, with 1 addedp(w \mid k) = \dfrac{\text{count}(w, k) + 1}{\sum_v \text{count}(v, k) + V}
Scorez_k = \log p(k) + \sum_i \log p(w_i \mid k)
Prediction and probabilities\hat{y} = \arg\max_k z_k, \qquad p(k \mid \text{text}) = \text{softmax}(z)_k

A word can be in the vocabulary yet never appear in one topic. Nash appears in business, entertainment and science articles, but never in sports.

The score of a topic is the log prior plus the sum of the logs of its word probabilities.

The largest score gives the prediction; softmax gives the probabilities.

The same steps in Python, on the 40,000 training articles.

counts = np.vstack([np.asarray(X_train[y == k].sum(axis=0)).ravel()
                    for k in range(6)])                   # 6 x V word counts
counts = counts + 1                                       # add 1
log_prior = np.log(np.bincount(y) / len(y))               # log p(k)
log_p = np.log(counts / counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))  # log p(w | k)
Z = log_prior + X_dev @ log_p.T                           # scores
pred = Z.argmax(axis=1)                                   # predictions
P = softmax(Z)                                            # probabilities

Loss in classification

In classification, the model gives a probability to every topic.

Star film wins

sports0.30
entertainment0.70true topic

p = 0.70

The loss depends only on the probability of the true topic.

A loss L(p) is a function of the probability p that must satisfy the following:

  1. L(1) = 0No loss when the true topic gets probability 1.
  2. L falls as p risesA larger probability gives a smaller loss.
  3. L rises as p fallsA smaller probability gives a larger loss.

What function can we use for the loss?

The simplest choice is L(p) = 1 - p, but it has several problems.

Cannot tell a confident error from an uncertain one

Model A is correct on all four texts. Model B is correct and very confident on three texts, but on Text 4 it is almost certain of a wrong answer.

With 1 - p, the mistake on Text 4 costs only 0.98, so Model B has the lower total loss.

Problem: with 1 - p, Model B has a lower loss than Model A. It should be the opposite, because Model A is correct on every text.

Training changes the model in small steps. In each step, every text pulls the model toward a higher p for that text.

How hard a text pulls is the slope of its loss. The graph of 1 - p is a straight line, so its slope is the same at every p.

The four texts pull equally hard, so the three right texts together pull three times as hard as Text 4.

Problem: with 1 - p, the mistake on Text 4 pulls no harder than a text that is already right.

Not additive over independent texts

We can score the two texts one at a time, or as one pair.

One at a time, each text costs 1 - 0.5 = 0.5, so the two texts cost 1.

Both texts are right with probability 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25, so the pair costs 1 - 0.25 = 0.75.

With more texts, each at p = 0.5, the gap grows. As one group, any number of texts costs less than 1.

Problem: with 1 - p, the loss of the same texts depends on how we group them. A good loss gives the same answer either way.

Bayes' formula gives the probability of a topic from the prior and the likelihood.p(k \mid \text{text}) = \dfrac{p(\text{text} \mid k)\, p(k)}{\sum_j p(\text{text} \mid j)\, p(j)}
A text is a bag of words. The naive assumption multiplies the probabilities of its words.p(\text{text} \mid k) = \prod_i p(w_i \mid k)
Logs prevent underflow, and softmax turns the scores back into probabilities.z_k = \log p(k) + \sum_i \log p(w_i \mid k)
p(k \mid \text{text}) = \text{softmax}(z)_k
The loss L(p) = -\log p is known as log loss, negative log-likelihood and, above all, cross-entropy.H(q, p) = -\sum_k q_k \log p_k = -\log p_{\text{true}}
