The chain rulep(w_1 \dots w_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(w_i \mid w_1 \dots w_{i-1})

In Naive Bayes, each word has its own probability, whatever comes before it.

To see order, the probability of each word must depend on the words before it.

The chain rule of probability writes the probability of a text exactly as this product.

Conditional probability

soccer: 171 times

p(\text{star}) = \dfrac{\text{count}(\text{star})}{\text{all words}} = \dfrac{489}{1{,}811{,}637} = 0.00027

The word soccer occurs 171 times in the 40,000 training texts.

In 10 of them, the next word is star.

So the probability of star, given soccer before it, is 10/171 = 0.058.

Without the word before it, star is 489 of the 1,811,637 words: a probability of 0.00027.

To compute a probability exactly, we count the history, and the history followed by the word.

Soccer occurs 171 times, and soccer star 10 times.

Soccer star george never occurs, so every longer history has a count of 0.

Problem: most long histories occur rarely or never in the training texts, so their probabilities are 0 or 0/0.

The Markov assumption

soccer star george weah wins liberian presidential election count 0 count 185
.
p(\text{election} \mid \text{soccer star george weah wins liberian presidential})
\approx p(\text{election} \mid \text{presidential})
p(w_i \mid w_1 \dots w_{i-1}) \approx p(w_i \mid w_{i-1})p(w_i \mid w_1 \dots w_{i-1}) \approx p(w_i \mid w_{i-N+1} \dots w_{i-1})
p(\text{election} \mid \text{presidential}) = \dfrac{\text{count}(\text{presidential election})}{\text{count}(\text{presidential})} = \dfrac{31}{185} = 0.17

The exact probability of election needs its whole history of seven words, which never occurs.

The Markov assumption keeps only the last words of the history. Here it keeps only the last one.

Presidential occurs 185 times, and 31 times the next word is election.

In general, each word depends only on the N − 1 words before it.

Solved: a short history occurs often enough in the training texts to be counted.

An n-gram is a sequence of n words. Single words are unigrams.

Two words are a bigram. A bigram model gives each word a probability from the word before it.

Three words are a trigram. A trigram model uses the two words before it.

A text of five words has five unigrams, four bigrams, three trigrams, two 4-grams and one 5-gram.

A trigram model gives each word a probability from the two words before it. The text starts with two <s>.

The likelihood multiplies these probabilities, one for each word and one for </s>.

Each probability is the count of three words divided by the count of the first two.

The prior, Bayes’ formula and the prediction stay as before.

With one word of history, each topic has a table. The sports articles of the toy example fill 13 of its 36 cells.

With two words of history, the table becomes a cube. The same articles fill 17 of its 216 cells.

In the C1 data, each topic's cube has 1.4 \times 10^{15} cells, but its training texts hold fewer than 300,000 different trigrams.

Problem: almost every cell of the cube is 0. Of the dev trigrams, 84.4% never occur in the training texts of their topic.

Three models in code

Unigram

for words, k in zip(train_words, y):
    for w in words:
        count[k][w] += 1
        total[k] += 1
p = (count[k][w] + 1) / (total[k] + V)

Bigram

for words, k in zip(train_words, y):
    words = ["<s>"] + words + ["</s>"]
    for v, w in zip(words, words[1:]):
        count[k][v, w] += 1
        total[k][v] += 1
p = (count[k][v, w] + 1) / (total[k][v] + V)

Trigram

for words, k in zip(train_words, y):
    words = ["<s>", "<s>"] + words + ["</s>"]
    for u, v, w in zip(words, words[1:], words[2:]):
        count[k][u, v, w] += 1
        total[k][u, v] += 1
p = (count[k][u, v, w] + 1) / (total[k][u, v] + V)

The unigram model counts each word in the training texts of its topic.

The bigram model adds <s> and </s>, and counts each word after the word before it.

The trigram model adds a second <s>, and counts each word after the two words before it.

All three add 1 to every count and V to every total, so an n-gram never seen still gets a small probability.

How good is a writer?

Mean loss per word\displaystyle \text{loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(w_i \mid \text{history})

Toy examplestarfilm→songp = 1/4, \quad \text{loss} = \log 4 = 1.39, \quad \text{perplexity} = 4

To score a writer, we give it new text, the dev articles, and look at the probability it gives each next word.

This is the loss of class 2, now on each next word, averaged over the 174,640 dev words.

Perplexity is e to the power of the mean loss. Lower is better.

After star film, the model chooses among four words with probability 1/4 each, so its perplexity is 4.

In entertainment, four of the six pairs that start with star never occur.

Add-one smoothing adds 1 to every count, so no count is 0.

The row total grows by V = 6, the number of columns: the five words and </s>.

Each new count divided by the new total gives a probability. The row still adds up to 1.

Solved: p(\text{wins} \mid \text{star}) = 1/12, so the headline Film star wins gets a probability above 0.

Backoff uses the trigram if it was seen, otherwise the bigram, otherwise the unigram.

The trigram panic among investors never occurs in the training texts.

The pair among investors never occurs either.

The word investors occurs 64 times, so backoff uses the unigram probability.

Add-one gives every word after panic among the same 1/110,609. Backoff gives investors 64/1,811,637, from its own count.

Interpolation instead always mixes the trigram, bigram and unigram probabilities, with weights that add up to 1.

Handling unseen n-grams

Method What it does Its weakness
Add-one Adds 1 to every count Gives far too much to unseen n-grams: 99.8% of the row after presidential
Add-k Adds a small k, chosen on the dev set One k for every row. Bigram dev loss: 10.68 at k = 1, 8.54 at k = 0.001
Backoff Uses the longest n-gram that was seen Drops context. To add up to 1, the longer n-grams must give up some probability

Every model must give unseen n-grams a probability above 0. Here are the three ways we saw.

Add-one gives every n-gram a probability above 0, but takes too much from the n-grams that were seen.

Add-k adds less. The best k is chosen on the dev set.

Backoff uses a shorter history when the longer one was never seen.

All three only repair counts. None of them knows that two words are alike.
