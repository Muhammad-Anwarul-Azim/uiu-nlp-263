Part 1: Text as vectors

Bag of words, TF-IDF and cosine similarity.

Bag of words

False{A, D}

  1. A

    “Bangladesh beat India” and “India beat Bangladesh” get different bag-of-words vectors, because their words come in a different order.

    False. Both sentences get the same vector: bangladesh 1, beat 1, india 1. The order is lost.

  2. B

    The text “dhaka beat india in dhaka” has 5 tokens and 4 types.

    True. The tokens are dhaka, beat, india, in and dhaka. The types are dhaka, beat, india and in.

  3. C

    Every bag-of-words vector has one position for each word of the vocabulary. With a vocabulary of 111,441 words, most of its entries are 0.

    True. A text uses only a few of the 111,441 words, so most of its counts are 0.

  4. D

    A word of a new text that is not in the vocabulary is counted at the position of the most similar vocabulary word.

    False. A word outside the vocabulary has no position, so it is skipped.

Term frequency and idf

Three texts: (1) “dhaka beat india in dhaka”, (2) “india beat dhaka in dhaka” and (3) “a new film in dhaka”. Here N = 3 and \mathrm{idf}(w) = \log\bigl(N / \mathrm{df}(w)\bigr).

False{A}

  1. A

    The term frequency of dhaka in text 1 is 3, the number of texts that contain dhaka.

    False. The term frequency is the count in the text, 2. The number 3 is the document frequency.

  2. B

    The idf of dhaka is \log(3/3) = 0, so its TF-IDF weight is 0 in every text, even in text 1, where its count is 2.

    True. A word in every text separates nothing: \mathrm{tf} \times 0 = 0.

  3. C

    The word beat is in two of the three texts, so its idf is \log 1.5 = 0.41.

    True. \mathrm{df} = 2, and \log(3/2) = 0.41.

  4. D

    If film occurred five times in text 3, its idf would still be \log 3 = 1.10.

    True. df counts texts, not occurrences. Film is still in one text, so \mathrm{df} = 1.

Cosine similarity

False{A, B, D}

  1. A

    Two texts with cosine 1 must have the same words in the same order.

    False. Cosine 1 means the same words in the same proportions, in any order, as texts 1 and 2 of question 2.

  2. B

    Writing a text twice, which doubles every count, changes its cosine with each topic average.

    False. The cosine does not change: \cos(2x, y) = \cos(x, y).

  3. C

    After each vector is scaled to length 1, the cosine of two vectors equals their dot product.

    True. \cos\theta = x \cdot y / (\lVert x \rVert \lVert y \rVert), and both lengths are 1.

  4. D

    The nearest topic average predicts the topic whose average has the smallest cosine with the text.

    False. It predicts the topic whose average has the largest cosine.

Part 2: Probabilities and loss

Naive Bayes, softmax and cross-entropy.

Bayes’ formula

False{D}

  1. A

    The prior p(k) is the share of training articles that belong to topic k.

    True. p(k) = n_k / N.

  2. B

    The naive assumption is that the words of a text are independent of each other, given the topic.

    True. This is why the likelihood is a product, \prod_i p(w_i \mid k).

  3. C

    The normalizer \sum_j p(\text{text} \mid j)\, p(j) is the same for every topic, so it does not change which topic is predicted.

    True. The largest posterior and the largest p(\text{text} \mid k)\, p(k) give the same topic.

  4. D

    If p(\text{text} \mid \text{sports}) = 0.003, p(\text{text} \mid \text{entertainment}) = 0.0105 and each prior is 0.5, the posterior of entertainment is 0.70.

    False. With priors of 0.5 the posterior is 0.0105 / (0.003 + 0.0105) = 0.78. The value 0.70 needs the priors 0.6 and 0.4.

Logs and softmax

False{B}

  1. A

    A product of 300 probabilities of about 0.002 is stored as 0 by the computer, but the sum of their logs, about -1864, is stored without trouble.

    True. The product is 0 from word 120 on, while 300 \log 0.002 = -1864.4.

  2. B

    The softmax gives probability 0 to a negative score.

    False. Every score gets a probability above 0, because e^{z} > 0.

  3. C

    The topic with the largest score z_k = \log p(k) + \sum_i \log p(w_i \mid k) is the topic with the largest product p(k) \prod_i p(w_i \mid k).

    True. The log is increasing, so the order of the topics does not change.

  4. D

    For the scores 2, 0 and -1, the softmax gives the first score the probability 0.84.

    True. e^{2} / (e^{2} + e^{0} + e^{-1}) = 7.39 / 8.76 = 0.84.

Cross-entropy

False{B, D}

  1. A

    Let q give probability 1 to the true topic. Then the cross-entropy H(q, p) = -\sum_k q_k \log p_k equals -\log p_{\text{true}}.

    True. Every term with q_k = 0 drops out.

  2. B

    Log loss, negative log-likelihood and cross-entropy are three different losses, and they give different values.

    False. They are three names for the same loss, -\log p_{\text{true}}.

  3. C

    The loss -\log p_{\text{true}} is 0 only when the model gives the true class probability 1.

    True. -\log p = 0 exactly when p = 1.

  4. D

    In a text, after a certain history, one word comes next in one third of the cases. The mean loss on this text is lowest when the model gives that word the probability 1 after that history.

    False. The mean loss is lowest at the probability 1/3, the word’s share in the text.

Writing with a unigram model

A naive Bayes table has one row of word probabilities p(w \mid k) for each topic k.

False{A, B, C, D}

  1. A

    Text written from the sports row keeps the word order of the sports training texts.

    False. Each word is drawn on its own, so the text has no word order.

  2. B

    To write, the model takes the word with the largest probability in the row at every step.

    False. A random number between 0 and 1 falls in one segment of the row, and that segment gives the word.

  3. C

    Naive Bayes can classify a text, but it cannot write one.

    False. Naive Bayes can also write: each row of its table is a unigram language model.

  4. D

    Counted without smoothing, the sports row can write a word that never occurs in the sports training texts.

    False. A word with count 0 has probability 0, so it is never drawn.

Part 3: N-gram models

Word order, generation and perplexity.

The Markov assumption

False\varnothing

  1. A

    The chain rule p(w_1 \dots w_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(w_i \mid w_1 \dots w_{i-1}) holds without any assumption.

    True. It follows from the definition of conditional probability.

  2. B

    A bigram model keeps only the word before: p(w_i \mid w_1 \dots w_{i-1}) \approx p(w_i \mid w_{i-1}).

    True. This is the Markov assumption with a history of one word.

  3. C

    A text of five words, without the marker <s>, has five unigrams, four bigrams and three trigrams.

    True. A text of n words has n - m + 1 n-grams of m words.

  4. D

    We make the Markov assumption because a short history occurs often enough in the training texts to be counted.

    True. Presidential occurs 185 times in the training texts, but soccer star george never occurs.

The bigram model

The marker <s> stands at the start and at the end of each text.

False{D}

  1. A

    A headline of three words gives four pairs.

    True. The pairs are <s> w_1, w_1 w_2, w_2 w_3 and w_3 <s>.

  2. B

    Bayes’ formula and the prior are the same as in naive Bayes; only the likelihood is new.

    True. Only the likelihood changes, to \prod_i p(w_i \mid w_{i-1}, k).

  3. C

    Without smoothing, a single pair that never occurs in training makes the probability of the whole headline 0.

    True. The likelihood is a product, and one of its factors is 0.

  4. D

    The probability of star after soccer is \mathrm{count}(\text{soccer star}) / \mathrm{count}(\text{star}).

    False. We divide by the count of the history: \mathrm{count}(\text{soccer star}) / \mathrm{count}(\text{soccer}) = 10/171.

Longer histories

False{B}

  1. A

    The longer the history, the larger the share of dev n-grams that never occur in the training texts.

    True. On the dev set, 3.5% of the words, 53.0% of the pairs and 84.4% of the trigrams are unseen.

  2. B

    Because it uses more context, the trigram model classifies the dev articles better than the bigram model.

    False. It classifies worse: a dev macro-F1 of 0.733, against 0.820 for the bigram model.

  3. C

    With add-one smoothing, an n-gram that never occurs in training gets almost the same probability in every topic, so it does not help to choose the topic.

    True. Its probability is close to 1/V in every topic.

  4. D

    Most of the different trigrams in the training texts occur only once.

    True. Of the different trigrams, 92.9% occur once.

Generation

False{C}

  1. A

    Generation is classification of the next word, with one class for each word of the vocabulary and one for <s>.

    True. In the C1 data the next word has 110,609 classes.

  2. B

    Classification gives one loss per article; generation gives one loss per word.

    True. Each word of the text is a true class, with its own loss -\log p.

  3. C

    The loss of a text is the product of the losses of its words.

    False. The loss of a text is the sum of the losses of its words, because the log of a product is a sum of logs.

  4. D

    A trigram model without smoothing writes only trigrams that occur in its training texts.

    True. Every other trigram has probability 0.

Perplexity

False{A, C}

  1. A

    Perplexity is e to the power of the total loss of a text.

    False. Perplexity is e to the power of the mean loss per word.

  2. B

    The next-word probabilities 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 give a perplexity of 4.

    True. The mean loss is (\log 2 + \log 4 + \log 8)/3 = 2 \log 2, and e^{2 \log 2} = 4.

  3. C

    A model that gives every true next word the probability 1 has perplexity 0.

    False. Its perplexity is 1, because its loss is 0 and e^{0} = 1.

  4. D

    To compare two models by their perplexity, we measure both on the same text.

    True. Perplexity depends on the text as well as on the model.

Part 4: The neural language model

The model of Bengio et al. (2003).

Words as vectors

In the model of Bengio et al. (2003), the table C has one row for each word of the vocabulary.

False\varnothing

  1. A

    The one-hot vectors of film and movie have dot product 0, the same as those of film and banana.

    True. Two different one-hot vectors never share a 1.

  2. B

    No column of C has a fixed meaning; its numbers are learned.

    True. We choose 30 columns, and training decides what they hold.

  3. C

    Lookup takes the row of C that belongs to a word. It gives the same result as multiplying the one-hot vector of the word by C.

    True. The one-hot vector has a single 1, so the product picks that row.

  4. D

    At the start of training every row of C holds random numbers; after training, similar words have similar rows.

    True. Words used in similar contexts end up with similar rows.

The forward pass

Bengio’s model reads a window of 4 words. Each row of C has 30 numbers, and the hidden layer has 100 units.

False{B, C, D}

  1. A

    The input x, the four rows of C joined end to end, has 120 numbers.

    True. 4 \times 30 = 120.

  2. B

    If the window grows from 4 words to 5, each row of C needs more numbers.

    False. C keeps 30 numbers per word. The input x grows to 150 numbers, and each row of H gets 30 more weights.

  3. C

    tanh brings every number of o between 0 and 1.

    False. tanh brings every number between -1 and 1.

  4. D

    The scores y must be positive before the softmax.

    False. A score can have any sign. The softmax uses e^{y}, which is always positive.

Softmax and the gradient

False\varnothing

  1. A

    The softmax P(i) = e^{y_i} / \sum_j e^{y_j} gives every word a positive probability, and the probabilities add up to 1.

    True. Every e^{y_i} is positive, and each is divided by their sum.

  2. B

    Because the softmax gives every word a probability above 0, the neural model needs no add-one smoothing.

    True. No probability is 0, so no loss is infinite.

  3. C

    The gradient of the loss with respect to the scores is the vector of probabilities with 1 subtracted at the true next word.

    True. \partial L / \partial y_j = P(j) - 1[j = w_t].

  4. D

    A training step subtracts from each parameter its gradient times the learning rate \varepsilon.

    True. \theta \leftarrow \theta - \varepsilon\, \partial L / \partial \theta.

Training the neural model

False{A, B, C, D}

  1. A

    After one training step, every row of C changes.

    False. Only the four rows of the words in the window change.

  2. B

    After one training step, only the row of U that belongs to the true next word changes.

    False. Every row of U changes, because the score of every word has a gradient.

  3. C

    Most of the parameters of the model are in C, the table of word vectors.

    False. Most are in U, which has 100 numbers per word, against 30 in C.

  4. D

    On the test text, the best n-gram model had a lower perplexity than the neural model mixed with a trigram model.

    False. The mixed model had the lower perplexity, 252 against 312.

Part 5: Word2vec

Skip-gram with negative sampling.

Neighbor and noise pairs

Word2vec, skip-gram with negative sampling, learns from pairs of a target word and a context word.

False{A, B, C}

  1. A

    With range 2, a target in the middle of a long sentence has two neighbor pairs.

    False. It has four neighbor pairs, two words on each side.

  2. B

    A larger range gives fewer pairs.

    False. A larger range gives more pairs, one for each word in the range.

  3. C

    A noise word is picked at random from the vocabulary, and it may be the target itself.

    False. A noise word is never the target itself.

  4. D

    In “at night the cat will sleep on the bed”, with range 2, the target night has three neighbor pairs.

    True. The pairs are (night, at), (night, the) and (night, cat). Only one word stands on the left of night.

Scoring a pair

False{A, B}

  1. A

    The dot product of a pair becomes a probability through the softmax over the whole vocabulary.

    False. The sigmoid turns it into the probability of yes, between 0 and 1. There is no softmax over the vocabulary.

  2. B

    The loss of a noise pair whose sigmoid value is s is -\log s.

    False. The right answer for a noise pair is no, so its loss is -\log(1 - s).

  3. C

    The target takes its vector from W, and the context word takes its vector from C.

    True. Every word has two vectors, one in W and one in C.

  4. D

    A neighbor pair with sigmoid value 0.182 has the error 1 - 0.182 = 0.818.

    True. The error is the label minus the sigmoid value, and the label of a neighbor pair is 1.

Updating the vectors

False{B, C}

  1. A

    In one step, the target vector w gets one push from each pair: the error of the pair times its context vector.

    True. w \leftarrow w + \eta \sum \text{error} \cdot c.

  2. B

    A noise pair has a negative error, so its push moves w toward its context vector.

    False. A negative error turns the push around, so w moves away from that context vector.

  3. C

    Each context vector is updated with the new w, after the target vector has moved.

    False. Every update uses the vectors from before the step, so it uses the old w.

  4. D

    One step uses one neighbor pair and its noise pairs, all with the same target.

    True. Each neighbor pair comes with its noise pairs, all with the same target.

What word2vec keeps

False{A, B, C, D}

  1. A

    With a single table of vectors, the pair (cat, cat) would get a low score, because cat is never beside cat.

    False. The score is v_{\text{cat}} \cdot v_{\text{cat}} = \lVert v_{\text{cat}} \rVert^2, which is high. Two tables let it be low.

  2. B

    After training, word2vec keeps the table C and discards W.

    False. Word2vec keeps W, and each row of W is the vector of a word.

  3. C

    Like Bengio’s model, word2vec stops when the loss on the validation text stops falling.

    False. It runs a fixed number of epochs, often 5, and \eta shrinks to zero.

  4. D

    Words with the same neighbors, such as cat and dog, end up far apart in W.

    False. They end up close, because the same context vectors pull them the same way.

Two tables

In a small text, cat and dog are found beside sleep and run, but never beside each other.

False{A, B, C}

  1. A

    Every word has two vectors because the target words and the context words come from two different vocabularies.

    False. Both tables have a row for every word of the same vocabulary. A word uses its row of W as a target and its row of C as a context.

  2. B

    One step on the pair (cat, sleep) changes w_{\text{cat}}, c_{\text{sleep}} and w_{\text{sleep}}.

    False. Only w_{\text{cat}} and c_{\text{sleep}} change. w_{\text{sleep}} is not in the pair, so it gets no push.

  3. C

    With one table, training on this text reaches the same loss as with two tables.

    False. After 20 passes the loss is 3.42 with one table and 0.29 with two.

  4. D

    The rows of W and C must have the same number of numbers, because the score of a pair is the dot product of a row of W and a row of C.

    True. A dot product needs two vectors of the same length.

Neighbor pairs and noise pairs

False{B}

  1. A

    In “the cat will sleep”, with range 2, (cat, sleep) is a neighbor pair, and so is (sleep, cat).

    True. Sleep is two words after cat. When sleep is the target, cat is two words before it.

  2. B

    The neighbor pairs come from text in which people have marked which words are related.

    False. They come from plain text with no labels. The text itself shows which words stand near which.

  3. C

    Without noise pairs, the loss could be brought close to 0 by giving every word the same long vector, in W and in C.

    True. Every score would be \lVert v \rVert^2, so every sigmoid would be near 1. The vectors would say nothing about the words.

  4. D

    A noise word is sometimes a real neighbor of the target. In a large text this is rare, so the pair is still treated as noise.

    True. With many thousands of words in the vocabulary, a random word is seldom a real neighbor.

The sigmoid of a score

The score of a pair is c \cdot w, and \sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x}).

False\varnothing

  1. A

    A pair with the score 0 has the sigmoid value 0.5, so yes and no are equally probable.

    True. \sigma(0) = 1/(1 + 1) = 0.5.

  2. B

    \sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x), so the probability of no for a pair with the score x is \sigma(-x).

    True. 1 - 1/(1 + e^{-x}) = 1/(e^{x} + 1) = \sigma(-x).

  3. C

    A neighbor pair and a noise pair both have the score 2. The noise pair has the larger loss, 2.13 against 0.13.

    True. \sigma(2) = 0.881, so the losses are -\log 0.881 = 0.13 and -\log 0.119 = 2.13.

  4. D

    If both vectors of a pair with the score 0.5 are doubled, its score becomes 2.

    True. (2c) \cdot (2w) = 4\,(c \cdot w) = 4 \times 0.5 = 2.

Raising and lowering a score

False{C, D}

  1. A

    Moving w a small step along c raises the score c \cdot w, and moving it against c lowers it.

    True. Adding a\,c to w, with a > 0, adds a \lVert c \rVert^2 to the score.

  2. B

    A noise pair with the score -5 has the error -0.007, so it hardly moves its vectors.

    True. \sigma(-5) = 0.007. The pair is nearly right, so its push is tiny.

  3. C

    A neighbor pair with a very large score has an error close to 1, so it gets the largest push.

    False. Its error 1 - \sigma is close to 0, so it hardly moves. The largest pushes go to the pairs that are most wrong.

  4. D

    One step on the pair (cat, sleep), whose sigmoid value is 0.69, makes its sigmoid value 1.

    False. The sigmoid never reaches 1. With \eta = 0.2, one step raised it from 0.69 to 0.75.

Why negative sampling?

The vocabulary has 3 million words, each vector has 300 numbers, and each neighbor pair comes with two noise pairs.

False{C}

  1. A

    With the full softmax, each step would need a score for every word of the vocabulary. With negative sampling it needs three scores.

    True. One score for the neighbor pair and one for each noise pair.

  2. B

    The scores of one step need 900 million multiplications with the full softmax, and 900 with negative sampling.

    True. 3{,}000{,}000 \times 300 = 900 million, and 3 \times 300 = 900.

  3. C

    Negative sampling gives the same probabilities of the next word as the softmax, only faster.

    False. Each pair gets its own probability of yes, and these need not add up to 1. Word2vec gives no probability of the next word.

  4. D

    Each step changes one row of W and three rows of C; every other row stays as it is.

    True. Only the rows in the three pairs get a push.

The noise distribution

False{A, C}

  1. A

    Every word of the vocabulary is drawn as a noise word with the same probability.

    False. A word is drawn with the probability \mathrm{count}(w)^{0.75} / \sum_{w'} \mathrm{count}(w')^{0.75}, so frequent words are drawn more often.

  2. B

    With the power 0.75, two words with the shares 0.99 and 0.01 of the text are drawn with the probabilities 0.97 and 0.03.

    True. 0.99^{0.75} = 0.992 and 0.01^{0.75} = 0.032, so 0.992 / 1.024 = 0.97 and 0.032 / 1.024 = 0.03.

  3. C

    The power 0.75 makes a rare word less likely to be drawn than its share of the text.

    False. The power makes a rare word more likely: 0.01 becomes 0.03.

  4. D

    The noise words are drawn from the same distribution for every target, except that the target itself is never drawn.

    True. The distribution depends only on the counts of the words.

The range of context

False{B, C, D}

  1. A

    A text of 9 words gives 16 neighbor pairs with range 1, and 30 with range 2.

    True. Range 1 gives 2 \times 8 = 16, and range 2 gives 2 \times (8 + 7) = 30.

  2. B

    When the range grows from 2 to 5, each row of W and C needs more numbers.

    False. The tables keep their size. Only the number of pairs grows, from about 4 to about 10 per word.

  3. C

    With a short range, the nearest vectors to a word belong to words on the same topic. With a long range, they belong to words of the same kind, which could take its place.

    False. It is the other way round. A short range gives words of the same kind; a long range gives words on the same topic.

  4. D

    A larger range always gives better vectors, because each target has more neighbors.

    False. The range is chosen on a dev set. A larger range also gives more pairs, so training takes longer.

Close vectors

In a small text, cat and dog are found beside sleep and run, but never beside each other. Word2vec is trained on it with two tables.

False\varnothing

  1. A

    Sleep and run end up with close rows of W, although (sleep, run) is a noise pair.

    True. Both are beside the same words, cat and dog. After 20 passes their rows of W have cosine 1.00.

  2. B

    Close rows of W for cat and dog do not give the pair (cat, dog) a high score.

    True. Its score is w_{\text{cat}} \cdot c_{\text{dog}}, which training pushes down, to -3.00 after 20 passes.

  3. C

    In a large text, two words can end up close even if they never occur in the same sentence, if each occurs beside the same words.

    True. Both words are pulled toward the same vectors of C, wherever they occur.

  4. D

    A word that occurs only a few times is in few pairs, so its row of W stays near its random start.

    True. A rare word gets few pushes, so its vector moves little and is less reliable.

What the vectors keep and lose

False{A, B, C, D}

  1. A

    Each sense of a word gets its own vector: bank of a river and bank for money get two rows of W.

    False. Each word has one row of W, whatever its sense. The vector of bank mixes its senses.

  2. B

    Words with opposite meanings, such as hot and cold, end up far apart, because their meanings differ.

    False. Hot and cold have the same neighbors, as in “the weather is hot”, so they end up close.

  3. C

    A pair records where its context word stood, so (cat, sleep) with sleep just after cat differs from (cat, sleep) with sleep two words after.

    False. The two are the same pair, with the same two vectors. The order within the range is lost.

  4. D

    The vector w_{\text{king}} - w_{\text{man}} + w_{\text{woman}} is exactly the vector of queen.

    False. The result is only nearest to queen. It is not equal to the vector of queen.

Word2vec and Bengio’s model

False{A, B, C, D}

  1. A

    Like Bengio’s model, skip-gram takes its context words only from before the target.

    False. Skip-gram takes its neighbors from both sides of the target. Bengio’s model reads only the words before.

  2. B

    Word2vec read about 70 times as much text as Bengio’s model.

    False. About 7,000 times: 100 billion words against 14 million.

  3. C

    Like Bengio’s model, word2vec passes the vectors through a hidden layer with tanh before it scores them.

    False. Word2vec has no hidden layer. The score of a pair is one dot product, c \cdot w.

  4. D

    For Bengio’s model the word vectors are the goal and the next word is only a means; for word2vec it is the other way round.

    False. It is the other way round. Bengio’s model is built for the next word; word2vec is built for the vectors.
