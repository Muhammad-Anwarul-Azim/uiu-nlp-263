At university you hear sentences all day: rules, promises, claims. Which of them can be called true or false? Call out true, false or neither before the slide moves. Three of the first four are Rosen’s Example 1 (p. 2): Washington true, 1 + 1 = 2 true, 2 + 2 = 3 false; the Chattogram sentence is ours, false. Those are propositions. The next three are Example 2 (p. 2). A question has no value. An instruction has no value. “x + 1 = 2” has a gap: no value until the gap is filled. A proposition is a sentence that is true or false, but not both.

Rosen, Example 4 (p. 3): “Vandana’s smartphone has at least 32 GB of memory.”

Put “not” in front of a sentence and the value flips. The book reads not-p as “it is not the case that p” (Definition 1, p. 3); in plain English we write “There is no quiz today.” Two rows, Table 1 (p. 4), and that is the whole table. One place students go wrong: the negation of “at least 32” is “less than 32” (Example 4, p. 3). “At most 32” is wrong, because 32 would make both sentences true, and a sentence and its negation can never both be true.

And

“But” is “and”.

“And” is strict. Both parts must hold; one false part makes the whole thing false. Call out each cell before it fills: T, F, F, F. That is Table 2 (p. 4). English “but” is the same connective with a note of surprise. “There is no quiz today, but I come to class” is not-p and q. Its one true row moves to the third case: no quiz, and I come.

“Or” allows both: the inclusive or. “Either … or …, but not both” is the exclusive or.

In logic, “or” means at least one. Both true is still true. Call the cells: T, T, T, F. That is Table 3 (p. 4). Sometimes English means exactly one. Rosen’s Example 9 (p. 6): the savings cannot pay for Europe and for the car. That is the exclusive or, with its own symbol, Table 4 (p. 6): F, T, T, F. The papers write it as “either … or …, but not both” (Rosen’s own phrase in Example 8, p. 6). The two tables differ in one row only, the first.

When is a promise broken?

“If I am elected, then I will lower taxes.”

p q p \rightarrow q
elected taxes lowered T T T kept
elected taxes not lowered T F F broken
not elected taxes lowered F T T nothing to test
not elected taxes not lowered F F T nothing to test

The conditional p \rightarrow q is false in one case only: p true and q false.

A politician promises: if I am elected, then I will lower taxes (Rosen p. 7). Go case by case. Elected and taxes lowered: the promise is kept. Elected and taxes not lowered: broken. That is the one false row. Not elected: the promise was never tested. Nothing is broken, so logic counts the sentence as true, whatever happened to taxes. That is the whole table of the arrow, Table 5 (p. 6): T, F, T, T. The arrow is false in one case only, p true and q false.

Hypothesis and conclusion

If there is a quiz today, then I come to class.   I come to class when there is a quiz today.

p q p \rightarrow q

The “if” part is the hypothesis, wherever English puts it.

Our class promise has the same shape. The arrow runs from the “if” part to the “then” part. Rosen calls them the hypothesis and the conclusion (Definition 5, p. 6). English often moves the “if” part to the end: “I come to class when there is a quiz today.” The sentence changes, the arrow does not. The broken case is the same: a quiz, and I am not there.

A false hypothesis makes the conditional true, whatever the conclusion.

The arrow does not ask whether the two parts are related. It only reads the table. “If 1 + 1 = 3, then 2 + 2 = 4” (Exercise 19 (b), p. 15): a false hypothesis and a true conclusion. The common answer is F. The table says T: nothing was promised, so nothing is broken. Change the conclusion to 2 + 2 = 5 (Exercise 19 (c)): both parts false, and the conditional is still true. Rosen’s remark on p. 8: the mathematical conditional needs no cause and effect; its truth values come from the table, not from English.

“You can receive an A in the course only if your score on the final is at least 90%.”

a: You receive an A.   s: Your score on the final is at least 90%.   (Rosen, p. 7)

an A at least 90% T T T

“X only if Y” is X \rightarrow Y.

Rosen’s own sentence (p. 7): “You can receive an A in the course only if your score on the final is at least 90%.” When is that broken? Go through the four cases. An A with at least 90%: fine. An A with under 90%: broken; the sentence said an A comes only with 90%. No A with at least 90%: allowed; 90% was never promised an A. No A, under 90%: fine. The one broken case is “an A” true and “at least 90%” false, so the arrow runs from the A to the score: a implies s. The part before “only if” is the hypothesis. The opposite arrow, from the score to the A, is wrong. Rosen’s warning on p. 7: be careful not to use “q only if p” to express p implies q, because this is incorrect. A score of 90% may or may not bring an A (row 3).

Necessary and sufficient

sufficientI am in Dhaka
necessaryI am in Bangladesh

If I am in Dhaka, then I am in Bangladesh.

Being in Dhaka is sufficient for being in Bangladesh.

Being in Bangladesh is necessary for being in Dhaka.

In Chattogram: in Bangladesh, not in Dhaka. The necessary condition alone is not enough.

The sufficient condition is the hypothesis. The necessary condition is the conclusion. One arrow.

Dhaka is inside Bangladesh. Being in Dhaka is enough to be in Bangladesh: sufficient. To be in Dhaka you must be in Bangladesh: necessary. Both sentences are the same arrow, from Dhaka to Bangladesh (Rosen p. 7: “p is sufficient for q” and “q is necessary for p” both express p implies q). Watch the dot move to Chattogram: in Bangladesh, and still not in Dhaka. The necessary condition alone does not take you to Dhaka. That is why necessary and sufficient are different words.

Unless

“I come to class whenever there is a quiz.”“I come to class unless it rains.”

q: I come to class.   r: It rains.

hypothesis\lnotthere is a quizit rains
conclusionI come to class

If it does not rain, then I come to class.   \lnot r \rightarrow q

Fno rain, and I stay away: broken

Tit rains, and I come anyway: not broken, nothing was promised for a rainy day

“q unless r” is \lnot r \rightarrow q. Unless means “if not”.

“Whenever” is “if” at the end of the sentence; you know it now: if there is a quiz, then I come. “Unless” hides a “not”. I come to class unless it rains: if it does not rain, then I come. The hypothesis is “it does not rain”. Rosen writes the rule as “q unless not p” for p implies q (p. 7); with r for the rain, “q unless r” is not-r implies q. The promise breaks when it did not rain and I stayed away. On a rainy day nothing was promised, so coming anyway breaks nothing. Two wrong forms students write, “rain implies not coming” and a two-way arrow, are the fourth item of the next quiz. Three of the last four mid-terms built question 1 on an “unless” sentence.

Turn the promise around

“The home team wins whenever it is raining.”

the statement   p \rightarrow q

If it is raining, then the home team wins.

converse: swap   q \rightarrow p

If the home team wins, then it is raining.

inverse: negate both   \lnot p \rightarrow \lnot q

If it is not raining, then the home team does not win.

contrapositive: swap and negate   \lnot q \rightarrow \lnot p

If the home team does not win, then it is not raining.

Which of the four say the same thing?

Rosen’s Example 12 (p. 9): “The home team wins whenever it is raining.” First the if-then form: if it is raining, then the home team wins. From one conditional you can make three more. Swap the two parts: the converse. Put “not” on both: the inverse. Do both: the contrapositive. Rosen names them on p. 9. Which of the four say the same thing? Hold that question for the next slide.

The Dhaka test

Tthe statementIf I am in Dhaka, then I am in Bangladesh.

FconverseIf I am in Bangladesh, then I am in Dhaka.

FinverseIf I am not in Dhaka, then I am not in Bangladesh.

TcontrapositiveIf I am not in Bangladesh, then I am not in Dhaka.

Only the contrapositive always agrees with the statement.

Test the four with a fact every student knows: Dhaka is inside Bangladesh. (The sentence is the colleague’s, C. A. Gomes, Propositional Logic, Part 2.) The statement is true. Move to Chattogram: in Bangladesh, not in Dhaka. The converse fails: I am in Bangladesh and not in Dhaka. The inverse fails for the same reason. Step outside Bangladesh: then you are certainly not in Dhaka. The contrapositive holds, and no place on the map can break it. Rosen, p. 9: only the contrapositive always has the same truth value as the statement.

One table for all four

the statement and its contrapositive: the same column in every row

the converse and the inverse: the same as each other, not as the statement

The same column in every row means equivalent.

Now the proof, for every case at once. Fill the four rows: TT gives T T T T; TF gives F T T F; FT gives T F F T; FF gives T T T T (check.py recomputes them). The rows TF and FT are where the columns differ. The first and the last column agree in every row: the statement and its contrapositive. The middle two agree with each other: the converse and the inverse. Two propositions with the same column in every row are called equivalent. That word carries the next three classes.

If and only if

“You can take the flight if and only if you buy a ticket.”

p q p \leftrightarrow q
flight ticket T T T
flight no ticket T F Fa free trip
no flight ticket F T Fturned away
no flight no ticket F F T
you take the flight you buy a ticket

p \leftrightarrow q  is  (p \rightarrow q) \land (q \rightarrow p)

The biconditional p \leftrightarrow q: two arrows, one promise each.

Rosen’s Example 13 (p. 10): “You can take the flight if and only if you buy a ticket.” A two-way promise. True when both happen or neither happens: T, F, F, T, Table 6 (p. 10). The two false rows are Rosen’s own cases: a flight without a ticket is a free trip; a ticket without a flight means the airline turned you away (Rosen says “bumped”). Each breaks one of the two arrows. p if and only if q is the same as (p implies q) and (q implies p). In mathematics we always say which arrow we mean; English often leaves the second one implicit (p. 10).

“There is a quiz today and I come to class, or I stay in the hall.”   r: I stay in the hall.

p \land (q \lor r)F

one case tells them apart: no quiz, I stay away, I am in the hall (p F, q F, r T)

When in doubt, write the brackets.

Symbols have an order, like multiplication before addition: not first, then and, then or, then the arrows (Table 8, p. 11; Rosen calls the connectives logical operators there). Not p and q means (not p) and q. p or q and r means p or (q and r). p or q implies r means (p or q) implies r; the other bracketing, p or (q implies r), differs when p is true and r is false. Without brackets, this English sentence has two readings. One case tells them apart: no quiz, I stay away, and I am in the hall. The first reading is true (r is true), the second false (p is false). Rosen keeps the brackets, and so do we. The sentence returns on Thursday.
